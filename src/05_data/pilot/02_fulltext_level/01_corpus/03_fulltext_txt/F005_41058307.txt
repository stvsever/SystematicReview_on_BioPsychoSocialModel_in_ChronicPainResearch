TITLE: Self‐Management Therapies for Temporomandibular Disorders—Evidence From Systematic Reviews

ABSTRACT:
Chronic pain in the orofacial region affects 10%–15% of the general population. It is most often related to temporomandibular disorders (TMD): pain in the masticatory muscles and the temporomandibular joints. Managing chronic pain conditions requires a biopsychosocial, evidence‐based and patient‐centred approach. Accordingly, self‐management has been advocated for TMD. This umbrella review aimed to evaluate existing evidence in systematic reviews (SRs) on self‐management therapy for TMD.
The review was carried out in accordance with the PRISMA and PRIOR guidelines, with a pre‐registered protocol (PROSPERO CRD42021276856). PubMed, Scopus, LILACS and the Cochrane Library were searched until December 1st 2023 to identify SRs that evaluated self‐management for TMD. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts, performed full‐text assessments, extracted all data and performed risk of bias assessment with AMSTAR 2.
A total of 1740 studies were identified. After title and abstract screening, 399 studies underwent full‐text assessment and 11 SRs comprising 49 unique primary studies were included. Of these, six SRs reported favorable results for self‐management for TMD, whereas five reported insufficient evidence either for or against the use of self‐management compared to other interventions. The overlap of primary studies between the SRs was 53%, and the main evidence gaps were related to quality of life and adverse effects outcomes. The methodological shortcomings of the SRs primarily stem from insufficient primary‐study design criteria or not providing references for excluded studies.
Existing evidence generally suggests beneficial effects from self‐management strategies such as patient education, behavioural therapy and jaw exercises.

FULL TEXT:
## Introduction

Temporomandibular disorder (TMD) is an umbrella term for musculoskeletal pain and jaw dysfunction in the orofacial region involving the jaw muscles, temporomandibular joints and surrounding tissues [ , ]. TMD is related to multiple consequences, among these a considerable impact on quality of life [ ]. Early identification and care of individuals suffering from TMD are essential for improving prognosis, reducing the risk of persistent pain and reducing the negative impact on quality of life [ ].

The prevalence of chronic TMD pain ranges from 10% to 15% in the general adult population, and women are more likely to be affected than men [ ]. This gender gap seems to increase for painful TMDs [ ] but not for temporomandibular joint‐related TMDs [ ]. Both the prevalence and incidence of TMD pain increase with age, peaking in middle age [ ], and the pain patterns commonly fluctuate over time [ ]. Thus, in a majority of TMD cases, in both adolescents and adults, the pain is not persistent over time but may be recurrent [ ]. Moreover, as with many other pain conditions, psychological comorbidities are common in TMD cases [ ], and psychosocial function correlates with the improvement, recurrence, or persistence of TMD symptoms [ ].

The aetiology of TMD is multifactorial and can be conceptualised within the biopsychosocial model, indicating an involvement of emotional, cognitive and behavioural factors [ ]. Given this multifactorial aetiology, effective TMD management should address these dynamically interacting factors contributing to the condition. To achieve this, management should adopt a biopsychosocial, patient‐centred and multimodal approach [ ].

Self‐management is considered a central part for patients with TMD and refers to patients themselves actively managing pain and its consequences, albeit with support from health care professionals. This treatment modality incorporates various behavioural change techniques that vary in type and quantity [ ]. Self‐management commonly includes patient education, relaxation techniques, parafunctional behaviour identification, monitoring and avoidance, dietary advice and home exercises [ ].

Multiple clinical trials evaluating self‐management interventions for TMD have supported the clinical use of this treatment modality. Moreover, scientific reports providing guidelines for the treatment of TMD emphasising self‐management have emerged in recent years [ ]. Nevertheless, they are largely consensus‐based, and evidence‐based guidelines for managing TMDs need updating [ ]. The development of such guidelines can be facilitated by well‐executed umbrella reviews synthesising the scientific knowledge on treatments for TMD.

Therefore, this umbrella review aimed to summarise the existing evidence in systematic reviews (SRs) on self‐management therapies for TMD. This can contribute to evidence‐based clinical practice guidelines, thereby supporting health care professionals' decision‐making. In addition, the results can contribute to recommendations for future clinical trials and for SRs on self‐management therapy for TMD.

## Method

This umbrella review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA [ ] and PRIOR guidelines [ ] and followed a study protocol registered in PROSPERO (CRD42021276856) prior to the review. This review is part of a larger overview project on the evidence for different therapies in the management of patients with TMD.

## Eligibility Criteria

SRs were included if they fulfilled the following inclusion criteria based on Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome and Study design (PICOS): Population : Any age groups (i.e., children, adolescents and adults). Any diagnostic criteria for TMD were allowed; however, the TMD condition had to be equivalent to the Diagnostic Criteria for TMD (DC/TMD) [ 1 ] or the Research Diagnostic Criteria for TMD (RDC/TMD) [ 2 ], specifically: myalgia, myofascial pain, arthralgia, osteoarthritis, osteoarthrosis, headache attributed to TMD, disc displacement with or without reduction and degenerative joint disease. SRs including only populations with a TMD diagnosis according to the extended DC/TMD [ 18 ] were excluded. Intervention : Self‐management strategies according to the proposed definition of the treatment modality [ 14 ]. Comparison : Any control or placebo interventions, including single or multiple treatment modalities. Outcomes : Pain intensity, mouth opening, global improvement, adverse effects, safety, or quality of life. Study design : SRs were included if they searched at least two databases and performed the selection of primary studies systematically. SRs were excluded if the full text was unavailable. The inclusion was limited to publications written in English, German, French, Italian, Spanish, Portuguese and Swedish.

## Information Sources and Search Strategy

Four electronic databases were initially searched in September 2021 and updated on December 1, 2023: PubMed (NLM), Scopus (Elsevier), LILACS (VHL), and Cochrane Library (Wiley). It was developed for PubMed in collaboration with an experienced research librarian at Malmö University and then adapted to the other databases. The search included a combination of free text and MeSH terms arranged in search blocks on TMD and SRs. No limitations in publication year or language were applied in the electronic search. Studies that were not on self‐management were excluded during the full text assessment. A hand search of the reference lists of the included SRs was carried out. Grey literature was not searched, and authors were not contacted for additional information. The full search strategy for all databases is provided in Appendix .

## Selection Process

After deduplication, all studies identified in the literature searches were imported to Covidence for study selection, data extraction and quality assessment. Two reviewers (TB and DD) independently screened titles and abstracts to assess potential eligibility. If at least one reviewer deemed a study to be of potential interest, it was retained for full‐text assessment. The same reviewers performed the full‐text assessment independently to determine whether the studies met the inclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus and through discussion with a third reviewer (RB or BHH).

## Data Collection Process

Data extraction was performed in an adjusted template in Covidence prior to the selection process of potentially eligible SRs. Two reviewers (TB and DD) independently extracted data from all the included SRs. The following data were extracted: first author, publication year, country, study funding, study aim, month and year of the latest database search, databases and additional searches, design of primary studies, diagnostic system (DC/TMD, RDC/TMD, or other), diagnoses, intervention(s), comparator/control(s), number of included participants, risk of bias assessment of primary studies, risk of bias assessment tool, follow‐ups, results, presence of a meta‐analysis and conclusion.

## Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias assessment of the included SRs was carried out in Covidence by two independent reviewers (TB and DD) using all 16 items of the AMSTAR 2 [ ]. Any disagreement between the reviewers was resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (RB or BHH).

## Synthesis Methods

In this umbrella review, we performed a qualitative synthesis of evidence from the included systematic reviews. The distribution in publication year of the included SRs was presented in a bar chart. We analysed the number of citations for each SR using Web of Science, and the five most cited SRs were presented in a table. The components of the self‐management intervention, comparators/controls and treatment outcomes were summarised narratively. Similar outcome measures were grouped to be descriptively compared across comparator interventions. Further, the applied risk of bias tool, the presence of a GRADE assessment and the AMSTAR 2 results were compiled for comparison between the studies.

The overlap of primary studies between eligible SRs was first presented as the ratio between the number of primary studies included more than once and the total number of primary studies. In addition, calculations were performed using the covered area (CA) and the corrected covered area (CCA) index methodology as proposed by Pieper et al. [ ] to quantify the degree of publication overlap: CA = N / r × c where CCA = N — r / r × c – r where N represents the total number of times primary studies appear in reviews, represents the total number of times primary studies appear in reviews, r is the number of unique primary studies and is the number of unique primary studies and c is the number of included systematic reviews. is the number of included systematic reviews.

A CCA of 0%–5% is considered as “slight overlap”, 6%–10% as “moderate overlap", 11%–15% as “high overlap”, and > 15% is considered as “very high overlap.”

The data on outcome measures used in the SRs and primary studies and the components of self‐management (patient education, cognitive–behavioural therapy, behavioural therapy and jaw exercises) examined in these studies were compiled and presented visually in an overview map.

## Reporting Bias and Certainty Assessment

Two reviewers (TB and DD) independently extracted the data and evaluated the risk of bias for each SR. Any disparity was resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (RB or BHH). After the data extraction process, all data were double‐checked by the reviewer TB, who compared the data against the full text of the SRs. In case of uncertainty, the extracted data were also checked by the reviewer BHH.

## Results

The literature search identified 2390 studies. After the removal of duplicates, 1740 abstracts were screened. Of these, 399 studies were retrieved in full text and assessed for eligibility. After the exclusion of 151 studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria at the full text assessment stage, the remaining 248 studies were categorised into five subgroups based on the main treatment evaluated: self‐management therapy; physiotherapy; occlusal splints, maxillofacial surgery and orthodontics; pharmacological treatments; and multiple or multimodal therapies. For the self‐management group, 11 SRs based on 49 unique primary studies were included (Figure ). Reference lists of the studies for which the full text was not found and the studies excluded during full‐text assessment are provided in Appendix . The most common reason for exclusion was the interventions not being self‐management therapies, followed by the studies not meeting the criteria for an SR (Table ). The included SRs were published between 2012 and 2023, and they were based on primary studies published between 1968 and 2020. Ten out of 49 primary studies were published within the last 10 years (Figure ).

A detailed description of the 11 included SRs is presented in Table . All included reviews were in English, and they were conducted in the United Kingdom [ , , , ], Brazil [ , ], Canada [ ], China [ ], Germany [ ], Italy [ ], and Japan [ ]. Five SRs had a pre‐registered study protocol [ , , , , ], and three SRs reported having received funding [ , , ]. Although three reviews did not report whether any funding was received [ , , ], no potential conflicts of interest for study authors of the reviews were identified.

Ten SRs included only randomized controlled trials, and five SRs classified TMD according to the RDC/TMD criteria. Eight reviews were based on primary studies with participants who had both muscle‐ and joint‐related TMD pain [ , , , , , , , ], and three reviews did not report the TMD diagnosis of participants in the reviewed primary studies [ , , ]. A majority of the SRs reported the total number of participants. Meanwhile, the participants' gender was clearly reported only in four of the included reviews, and these had predominantly female participants [ , , , ]. Six SRs were based on adult study populations only [ , , , , , ], four included adolescents and adults [ , , , ], and one included only adolescents [ ]. Apart from the most cited SR [ ], which had more than 170 citations, the number of citations for the other SRs was considerably lower (Appendix ).

The evaluated self‐management therapy varied in both number and type of therapy components. The number of components in the self‐management interventions ranged from one to five (Table ). Behavioural therapy in combination with patient education and/or cognitive behavioural therapy formed part of the self‐management intervention in all but one SR, which was also the only SR that evaluated only one component of self‐management [ ]. Jaw exercises were included in the self‐management interventions in the majority of SRs, and in two reviews, they were also combined with physiotherapy. The comparison interventions mainly consisted of patient education and/or behavioural therapy in combination with different treatments, such as intraoral appliance, biofeedback and pharmacological treatment. Six SRs compared self‐management to no treatment. The follow‐up periods varied from immediately after treatment completion to 12 months post intervention.

## Risk of Bias Assessment

All SRs showed some concerns based on the AMSTAR 2 assessment, although the number of concerns varied (Table ). Only one review did not report potential conflicts of interest [ ], and only one review reported sources of funding for the included primary studies [ ]. In a majority of the SRs, the concerns were related to not explaining the selection of study design or not providing a list of excluded studies with reasons for exclusion. Four SRs performed a meta‐analysis, but one of them did not assess the potential impact of the risk of bias in primary studies on the results of the analysis. All but one of the SRs performed a quality assessment of the primary studies, a majority of them using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 tool. A GRADE assessment was conducted in five of the included reviews.

## Overlap of Primary Studies

The number of relevant primary studies included in the individual SRs ranged from 1 to 22. Ten SRs were based on primary studies that were also included in the other SRs (Table ). Of the 49 included primary studies, 26 were reviewed in more than one SR, corresponding to an overlap of 53%. Expressed as CA, the percentage of overlap is 18%. The degree of overlap when calculated according to the CCA formula was 9%.

## Treatment Effect and Evidence Map

Six SRs reported favourable results for self‐management therapy for TMD [ , , , , , ] when compared to intraoral appliances [ , , ], no treatment or education only [ ], or usual care (comprising a combination of multiple interventions) [ ]. One of the SRs found self‐management to be effective in reducing pain and improving function without specifying the comparison intervention [ ]. Five SRs concluded that there was insufficient evidence either for or against the use of self‐management therapies over other interventions or control groups [ , , , ]. Five SRs performed a GRADE assessment, four of which reported low or very low certainty of evidence for the outcome of pain intensity when self‐management was compared to other interventions (Table ). A weak level of evidence was also found for the outcomes maximal mouth opening [ ] and quality of life [ ], and a very low certainty of evidence for adverse effects [ ].

The main components of self‐management therapy for TMD in the included SRs and their primary studies were patient education, behavioural therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy and jaw exercises. Patient education and behavioural therapy were part of self‐management therapy for TMD in 38 of the 49 primary studies reviewed in eight SRs. Almost half of the primary studies (24 out of 49 incorporated in seven SRs) evaluated cognitive behavioural therapy as part of a self‐management intervention or as a single treatment. In total, 20 primary studies in eight SRs evaluated jaw exercises.

All SRs, based on 43 of the 49 primary studies, included pain intensity as an outcome measure. Six reviews also evaluated treatment effect as the change in maximal mouth opening capacity, based on 20 primary studies. Quality of life and adverse effects were less commonly used as outcome measures. Among all primary studies, four included quality of life as an outcome measure and five reported the adverse effects of self‐management.

The largest amount of evidence, with a total of 10 SRs including 33 primary studies, evaluated the effect of patient education and behavioural therapy on pain intensity. The least evaluated components of self‐management were cognitive–behavioural therapy and jaw exercises; no primary studies or SRs evaluated the effects of jaw exercises on quality of life (Figure ).

Based on age groups, nine out of the 49 primary studies included adolescents in their study population; seven of these involved adult participants, whereas two were carried out with adolescents only. No primary study included children. Jaw exercises were the most common self‐management therapy component evaluated in these primary studies, followed by behavioural therapy. Pain intensity and maximal mouth opening capacity were the main outcome measures used in the primary studies including adolescents.

## Discussion

This umbrella review synthesised evidence from 11 SRs encompassing 49 unique primary studies to evaluate the effect of self‐management interventions for TMD. Our findings suggest that self‐management strategies—including patient education, behavioural therapy, cognitive‐behavioural therapy and jaw exercises—are effective in reducing pain and improving function in individuals with TMD but that quality of life and adverse effect outcomes are less often reported.

The first studies on self‐management for TMD were published already in the 1960s [ ], whereafter research in this field increased in the 1990s, during the same period that initiatives were taken toward a biopsychosocial approach on TMD. Nonetheless, the standardisation of biopsychosocial management protocols has been lagging behind and is related to uncertainty in the treatment of TMD [ , ]. Consequently, publications evaluating the effect of different interventions have been heterogeneous. Similarly, our umbrella review found a variation among the included SRs regarding the number and type of self‐management therapy components evaluated, the comparison interventions and the follow‐up periods. However, this review also identified an agreement among SRs that self‐management for TMD often includes several components and constitutes a multimodal treatment strategy, a finding in line with international consensus [ ]. The first stage of such multimodal self‐management interventions often incorporates patient education.

Providing education about the nature of TMDs and providing a diagnosis to patients has been recommended as part of the management of patients with TMD [ ]. Patient education alone or as part of cognitive–behavioural therapy formed part of the self‐management intervention in all but one SR included in this umbrella review, indicating the established role of this therapy component for TMD. Furthermore, in relation to the publication year of the primary studies, patient education was included in the intervention relatively evenly between the years 1968 and 2020. The patient education comprised structured information about the multifaceted aspects of TMD aetiology, including the biopsychosocial model; the rationale for self‐management; treatment methods; and prognosis, verbally provided by different dental professions (dental nurse, hygienist, general dental practitioner, or specialist in orofacial pain) and psychologists. In most of the primary studies, patient education was one of several evaluated therapies and was also the most used comparator intervention in both the SRs and primary studies, further suggesting that reconceptualising patients' understanding of their pain constitutes a basic and essential part in managing TMD. Thus, the findings of this umbrella review support patient education as a therapeutic strategy and reinforce research findings on this therapy for chronic pain conditions [ ]. Education has been found to reduce kinesiophobia and pain catastrophising and facilitate patients' ability to cope with their chronic pain condition [ ]. Importantly, evidence suggests that education alone is not sufficient to achieve clinically relevant and long‐lasting improvements in pain and pain‐related disability [ ]; education should be combined with other treatment modalities, and this is also consistent with the biopsychosocial framework [ ]. Hence, the inclusion of pain education as one of several therapy components in a self‐management intervention for TMD is scientifically supported.

Behavioural therapy for TMD is a self‐management therapy component that focuses on identifying, monitoring and avoiding behaviours disadvantageous to the rehabilitation of the jaw system and simultaneously reinforcing advantageous behaviours. Several clinical trials report favourable outcomes from behavioural therapy as part of self‐care interventions for TMD [ ]. However, behavioural therapy is rarely evaluated as the only intervention: reported treatment outcomes cannot be exclusively attributed to this therapy component but should be viewed as compound outcomes of a multimodal intervention. The earliest identified primary study evaluating behavioural therapy in the included SRs was published in 1984 [ ], whereafter a relatively even publication rate is observed. Most primary studies were randomised controlled trials in adult populations where the behavioural therapy was delivered in live individual or group sessions and comprised a biobehavioural approach that included several components.

Counselling is one recurring behavioural therapy component that usually forms part of self‐care interventions for TMD. In the primary studies, counselling included advice about relaxation techniques, deep breathing, sleep hygiene, therapeutic exercises, thermal therapy, self‐massage and a pain‐free diet. Since there is no clearly defined distinction between the therapeutic content of self‐management therapy components, counselling frequently serves as a therapeutic bridge between behavioural therapy and patient education [ ], with both being components of cognitive behavioural therapy for chronic pain . .

Cognitive behavioural therapy is a collective term for psychotherapies that combine behavioural principles with cognitive therapies to generate a range of therapeutic approaches. Since the 1960s, cognitive behavioural therapy has been applied for chronic pain conditions and undergone several phases of development [ ]. Today, there is scientific support for cognitive behavioural therapy as a treatment modality for managing chronic pain conditions, albeit with varying success [ ]. The present umbrella review found that clinical trials on cognitive behavioural therapy for TMD treatment were predominantly conducted and published in the 1990s or early 2000s, whereafter the scientific interest in evaluating this intervention has somewhat decreased. The summarised evidence on the effect of cognitive behavioural therapy in the treatment of TMD was limited, with low‐certainty evidence of improvements in pain intensity. Nonetheless, the findings suggest that cognitive behavioural therapy as part of a self‐management intervention for TMD may improve psychological outcomes. Considering the comorbidity of various psychological conditions and TMD [ ] and the consensus on advocating patient‐centred treatments with a biopsychosocial approach [ ], cognitive behavioural therapy is expected to form a critical part of future multimodal and interdisciplinary TMD management interventions. In the literature, cognitive behavioural therapy, behavioural therapy and patient education are sometimes referred to as psychological interventions, and a recently published meta‐analysis has shown them to be equivalent to standard TMD treatment regarding pain relief in painful TMD [ ].

Another treatment modality that forms part of the standard TMD treatment is various jaw exercises; it generally aims to reduce pain and improve function by attaining the rehabilitation and relaxation of tender masticatory muscles. The outcome of jaw exercises depends on the type of exercise prescribed and the corresponding mechanism of action. Nevertheless, research has suggested that therapeutic jaw exercises are effective in decreasing pain intensity and improving jaw function [ ]; in addition, they are a cost‐effective therapy of choice in patients with TMD pain [ ]. Evidence from the present umbrella review shows that jaw exercises as part of self‐management interventions for TMD are effective in terms of pain relief and jaw mobility. The types of jaw exercises evaluated in the SRs were diverse and included stretch, excursion, resistance and coordination exercises in various protocols, frequencies and durations. Most SRs reported jaw exercises as part of self‐management interventions for adult populations with TMD, but individual primary studies also evaluated the effect of jaw exercises as a stand‐alone therapy. Overall, the number of primary studies and SRs evaluating jaw exercises for TMD is still limited and the data from clinical trials is heterogenous, making it difficult to reliably synthesise existing evidence and to compare the effect of jaw exercises to similar treatment modalities for other chronic pain conditions. Nonetheless, research clearly supports physical activity as an effective treatment for chronic pain [ ], including TMD‐related pain according to the findings of the present umbrella review.

To promote and facilitate the clinical implication of effective self‐management strategies, future research should aim to provide guidance on which self‐management components have the most effect. With patient education and behavioural therapy being the most evaluated self‐management modalities, there is a need for additional studies evaluating the effect of CBT and jaw exercises on TMD. However, this demand must be met with research of improved methodological design aimed at the younger population, where the amount of evidence is generally most limited. In particular, studies investigating the effect of individual components of self‐management in children with TMD are recommended. Furthermore, future research should evaluate the effects of education and cognitive behavioural therapy on adolescents with TMD, preferably including quality of life and adverse effects among the outcome measures. Although most of the primary studies are randomised controlled trials, the included SRs identified several methodological weaknesses in these studies, and the certainty of evidence for reported findings was low to very low. In addition, the quality assessment of the included SRs displays several methodological concerns. Consequently, interpretations of reported findings should be made cautiously. Despite these shortcomings, the beneficial effects of self‐management for TMD found in this umbrella review are well in line with research findings for other chronic pain conditions. There is growing empirical and scientific support for self‐management in the treatment of various chronic pain conditions in both youth and adults [ , ], and the findings of this umbrella review contribute to this evidence.

## Strengths and Limitations

This umbrella review has several strengths, including a thorough and broad search strategy, generous inclusion criteria, and a study selection and data extraction performed by two independent reviewers. The review was based on a detailed pre‐registered protocol before the search process. One limitation was the moderate overlap of primary studies between the SRs. Overlap between studies poses a challenge when summarising evidence; namely, it increases the risk of overstating sample sizes and effects, resulting in false effect sizes and conclusions. We did not conduct a pooled meta‐analysis of data on the effect of self‐management on outcomes of interest for several reasons, including the degree of publication overlap and the observed heterogeneity. Variations in sample sizes, the use of diagnostic criteria for TMD, the number and type of self‐management therapy components, the comparator intervention(s), and the follow‐up period in the primary studies all contributed to the large degree of heterogeneity found in this review. Another factor was the methodological quality of the primary studies. The low confidence in the body of evidence from clinical trials reported in the available GRADE assessments in combination with findings from risk of bias assessments in the remaining SRs entailed a judgement of a high risk of biased estimates and misleading conclusions if a meta‐analysis was to be conducted.

All included SRs showed some concerns according to AMSTAR 2. In most SRs, this was partly due to them not providing a full list of excluded studies with reasons for exclusion. In addition, the quality assessment found limited reporting of details of the included primary studies in the SRs, and in most cases, information about the clinical setting of these studies was missing. SRs including primary studies with a high risk of bias were not excluded from this umbrella review; however, we endeavoured to make it visible to the readership by emphasising such data in the qualitative synthesis of evidence. Although this review includes SRs with an overall acceptable methodology based on AMSTAR 2, the evidence from the corresponding primary studies does not reflect equivalent methodological strength, and the number of primary studies is also limited. Therefore, the quality and quantity of clinical trials evaluating the effect of self‐management for TMD require improvement.

## Conclusion

Current evidence suggests that self‐management therapy can be effective in reducing pain and improving jaw function in patients with TMD, especially in an adult population treated with the therapy components patient education and behavioural therapy. There is limited evidence available to draw conclusions regarding the effect of self‐management interventions on quality of life or adverse effects. In line with recommendations to use self‐management as the first line of treatment, existing evidence suggests beneficial effects from self‐management strategies such as patient education, behavioural therapy and jaw exercises. Future SRs should also incorporate quality of life and adverse effect outcomes in order to provide a more comprehensive overview with a focus on patient perspectives.
