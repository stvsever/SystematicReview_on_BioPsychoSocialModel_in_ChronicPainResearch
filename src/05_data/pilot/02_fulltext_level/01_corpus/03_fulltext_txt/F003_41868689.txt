TITLE: Acceptance of pain neuroscience education in chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions: qualitative systematic review

ABSTRACT:
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.
Six factors influence the acceptance of pain neuroscience education: clear communication, biomedical beliefs, personal relevance, tailored setting, strong clinician relationships, provider confidence, and role clarity.
Pain neuroscience education (PNE) is a recognized strategy to manage biopsychosocial aspects of chronic pain. To this date, a systematic synthesis of determinants affecting the use and acceptance of PNE in clinical practice is pending. Primary qualitative articles were included if they investigated patient and/or provider perspectives on the use and acceptance of PNE in chronic musculoskeletal pain management. We searched MEDLINE (PubMed), Web of Science, and PsycInfo to identify eligible articles. Two researchers independently conducted the consensus-guided study selection process. Methodological quality was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute's (JBI) critical appraisal tool for qualitative research. Data from retrieved articles were synthesized using the JBI meta-aggregation approach. Confidence in findings was assessed by the ConQual approach to synthesized findings. Sixteen studies were included for qualitative meta-aggregation, comprehending perspectives of 329 participants. The analysis revealed 6 synthesized findings: (1) Basic didactic principles such as metaphors, multichannels, repetition, and plain messages support the delivery of PNE; (2) persisting beliefs in biomedical explanations of pain can lead to skepticism and misunderstandings of PNE; (3) emphasize the benefits of PNE and create personal relevance; (4) a group vs an individual setting should be chosen according to patient needs; (5) a good clinician–patient relationship is crucial for the acceptance of PNE; and (6) lacking confidence, unclear responsibilities, and a professional culture focused on biomedical aspects of care can keep practitioners from delivering PNE. Addressing these findings will contribute to a wider use and acceptance of PNE clinical practice.

## 2. Methods

This qualitative systematic review was registered on Open Science Framework ( https://osf.io/cf7r3 ). In addition, a comprehensive study protocol has been published. ). In addition, a comprehensive study protocol has been published. 20

## 2.1. Review question(s)

(1) Which barriers and facilitators to the use and acceptance of PNE do healthcare providers experience in the management of chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions? (2) Which barriers and facilitators to the use and acceptance of PNE do patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions experience?

## 2.2.1. Participants/population

In this review, we included all qualitative studies conducted with healthcare providers involved in the management of chronic musculoskeletal pain. This can involve all relevant physician specialty groups (ie, family physicians, orthopedic specialists, neurologists, or psychiatrists) and care providers of psychology, nursing, physiotherapy, sports therapy, occupational therapy, osteopathy, or chiropractic care. Eligible articles must address healthcare providers' experiences, perspectives, and attitudes toward PNE use, and/or its acceptance and applicability in the management of chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions.

Moreover, qualitative studies were included focusing on patient experiences, perspectives, and attitudes toward PNE use, acceptance, and applicability. Eligibility of studies investigating patient perspectives was given if participants were diagnosed with a chronic musculoskeletal health condition, such as chronic low back pain, chronic pain in the joints of the upper or lower limb (ie, frozen shoulder, epicondylitis lateralis), fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, osteopenia, gout, or juvenile arthritis. On the other hand, ineligible were patient groups diagnosed with chronic cancer pain, chronic postsurgical pain, and chronic pregnancy-related pain, as these groups are typically not targeted to PNE content. Studies involving children and adolescents were excluded as PNE for pediatric populations represents a conceptually distinct educational domain, characterized by different cognitive, developmental, and contextual requirements. Including these populations would have introduced substantial heterogeneity and limited the applicability of synthesized results to adult clinical practice.

## 2.2.2. Phenomena of interest

We included qualitative studies in which perspectives on educational interventions about the biology and physiology of pain were targeted. Within these educational interventions, an improved understanding of pain origins, biopsychosocial aspects of its etiology, and underlying biological functions had to be central concepts of information provision. In that respect, eligible educational interventions facilitate a shift of patients' perspectives on pain, representing an indication of tissue damage toward pain representing a personal need to protect the body. Following this understanding of PNE, we excluded studies targeting perspectives on multifaceted and multidisciplinary pain management interventions (ie, a combination of exercise approaches, manual therapy, psychological interventions, education, pharmacological interventions, coping interventions such as relaxation, imagery, or self-management skills) when PNE was embedded as one of multiple therapeutic components. In these contexts, participants' views could not be analytically isolated to PNE itself, and inclusion would have risked compromising construct specificity by conflating PNE-related determinants with those attributable to other elements of the intervention.

## 2.2.3. Context

We included qualitative studies investigating patient and healthcare provider perspectives in musculoskeletal care settings in primary, secondary, or tertiary care. We excluded settings of palliative and intensive care units and emergency departments as we expected the concept of PNE to be subordinate to these contexts.

## 2.2.4. Types of studies

This review included only original qualitative research comprising interview studies, focus group studies, observational ethnographic studies, or distinctly reported qualitative results of mixed-methods studies. Other forms of narrative literature, such as opinion articles, editorials, or expert articles, were excluded from analysis. This limitation of eligible research was determined to ensure methodological rigor in the exploration of opinions, motives, and social norms regarding the acceptance, use, and applicability of PNE in the management of chronic musculoskeletal pain conditions.

## 3.4.1. Synthesized finding 1: basic didactic principles such as metaphors, multichannels, repetition, and plain messages support the delivery of pain neuroscience education

To make PNE content understandable and internalize PNE messages, health care professionals (HCPs) should take enough time and use basic didactic principles such as metaphors, repetition, and easily understandable messages. A standard mode of PNE delivery is through video. However, this format can be perceived as tedious and exhausting. This seems to be especially prevalent in video formats in which messages are difficult to understand, and comics and cartoons illustrate protagonists. On the other hand, easily understandable messages that are convincingly presented as evidence-based help the trustworthiness of PNE videos. Independent of the delivery format, longer PNE sessions can be perceived as overwhelming. “Other than looking at those videos that we had to watch, they were a bit tedious (…). Well, I mean, we covered it all in with [the Physiotherapist]. We sort of talked about it, that one about, I don't know, deflecting pain, imagine pain, the worst and I don't know that one. I actually didn't even get all the way through that.” [Patient] 3 “Too much, too quick.” [Patient] 50

On the other hand, HCPs find metaphors helpful for explaining chronic pain mechanisms, such as comparing pain with a car's petrol light, making it easier for patients to understand. Patients use metaphors, like comparing chronic pain with a faulty car or smoke alarm, to communicate and help others understand their pain. Generally, patients value the language of PNE, as it offers terms to express their pain experiences better. Moreover, patients value the concept that their body is “safe,” although they experience pain, although it can be a difficult idea to internalize. In that respect, patients can find it helpful to understand their pain as a bodily protection mechanism that can become faulty or overprotective. “The tank and the light, that was a good one too. they often recognize it, that the petrol light comes on, you fill up the tank and you drive away, and the light comes on again. That with the wiring, that you have to look higher up, I think, yes that's a recognizable explanation for many people.” [General Practitioner] 9 “My body is actually safe even though it feels so painful. I still have to tell myself this sometimes because it is a totally foreign concept.” [Patient] 21

In-person PNE sessions, which are accompanied by PNE video content, help reinforce and repeat the information shared by HCPs, aiding in better understanding for patients. In addition, providing written materials for patients to review at home helps them process and better understand new information about their chronic pain condition. When HCPs use consistent language, it helps patients internalize and accept PNE messages, preventing confusion and enhancing the effectiveness of educational content. Furthermore, HCPs frequently make use of reassurance, which helps reduce anxiety and encourages their clients to engage in movement and return to daily life activities. “I am very satisfied with the psychosomatic physiotherapists who work in this region. We've agreed to speak the same language, so that people are told one thing, otherwise it can get confusing. And that generally works well.” [General Practitioner] 9 “You can reassure them really well … the signs are that there is nothing seriously wrong … what I did with him, was just reassure him that nothing serious was wrong. And that as long as he was willing to move and you know try and get back to a normal pattern at work he would be absolutely fine.” [Physiotherapist] 56

Patients can find information about pain neurophysiology to be complex and boring, struggling to understand the scientific details without prior knowledge. They also face challenges in applying pain neurophysiology concepts to their daily lives, especially when trying to shift their mindset and behaviours based on this information. In that respect, patients find it also difficult to communicate pain as a conceptual idea, as they experience it personally, making it harder to convey in abstract terms. “All that stuff with the brain, how it all works, that is difficult, you know, if you don't have the knowledge, I find it very difficult, I don't understand it all that fast, it's all so complicated.” [Patient] 36

## 3.4.2. Synthesized finding 2: persisting beliefs in biomedical explanations of pain can lead to skepticism and misunderstandings of pain neuroscience education

Persisting beliefs in biomedical explanations of chronic pain represent major barriers to an in-depth understanding and acceptance of the PNE concept. Understanding that pain originates from the brain rather than physical structures can lead to the perception that pain is “invented” or not real, although the person still feels the pain as genuine. The idea that pain can result from a heightened nervous system response may cause resistance, as some individuals find it hard to accept or understand. Explaining pain through the PNE approach may lead to patients feeling that their pain is not being taken seriously, as it can be perceived as psychological or imagined. “I do not agree that it is caused here (he points to his head), I can hardly understand this. I can't believe this, maybe the doctor is right, but I look at it differently” [Patient] 36

Patients often found educational content on exercises aimed at desensitizing pain (such as meditation and breathing exercises) to be unhelpful, frustrating, and not in line with their practical approach to pain management. To better accept PNE, patients required more precise explanations about the link between chronic pain and mental health, emphasizing the emotional toll of chronic pain. Despite that, many patients appreciate that thoughts, beliefs, and emotions can influence their pain experience. HCPs experience that chronic pain patients often have comorbid depression, which potentially hinders the acceptance of PNE, as the emotional burden may overshadow the focus on pain management strategies. Moreover, some patients express hesitation or misconceptions about psychological assessments, often thinking they are only for those who are severely depressed or “crazy.” “That's often the story with chronic people that you end up, you know they're so depressed and low that you feel a bit like the Dementors in Harry Potter, you feel like your soul is being sucked out of you because you're constantly trying to kind of buffer them up … So, you've got chronic problems and they're depressed … they can't play golf, they can't do their shopping, they can't drive very far, they're not working … you cry with people sometimes, it's though … with him I did get a bit frustrated … he seemed a bit needy.” [Physiotherapist] 56

HCPs note that patients sometimes perceive concepts such as central sensitization as a form of illness, mainly when explained regarding faulty body systems such as “wiring.” This can lead to confusion or concern about something being wrong with them. Moreover, strong beliefs in biological causes of chronic pain may prevent patients from accepting concepts of PNE, which suggests a more complex, multifactorial origin of pain. In that respect, patients find it helpful to shift the focus from blaming specific body parts (such as an arm) to understanding pain stemming from the central nervous system, offering a new perspective on managing pain. Without medical examinations or precise physical diagnoses, patients often struggle to accept that their chronic pain is not linked to a physical problem, despite reassurances that there is nothing seriously wrong.

Irrespective of this, religious beliefs and practices, such as using herbs or seeking spiritual healing, can play a role in how patients experience and manage chronic pain. “The tricky thing is, that if you say there's something wrong with the wiring, the inhibitory wiring, it doesn't function as it should, but it can get better, that you still call it a kind of illness. “O, so I have faulty wiring, so something is wrong”. That's what people may think, now we're talking about it, I think that's a difficult one.” [General Practitioner] 9 “They use sometimes some herbs to manage their pain. They use Hijama, it's like cupping and other religious things for pain. They go to someone who does that, and they think that it can heal.” (C) [Physiotherapist] 34 “Okay, so it's chronic pain. That is also reassuring in a sense that there is nothing going on. It's really double! Because neurologically there is nothing wrong, somehow you don't trust that you've not had scans made and no real medical examinations were done. On the other hand, it's comforting to hear that there is nothing serious going on.” [Patient] 57

## 3.4.3. Synthesized finding 3: emphasize the benefits of pain neuroscience education and create personal relevance

In synthesized finding 3, it is highlighted that HCPs should emphasize the benefits of PNE and create personal relevance to their patients. Patients regularly feel that gaining a conscious understanding of chronic pain mechanisms does not significantly change their lived experiences of pain, viewing it more as a theoretical understanding rather than something that improves their daily life. “It was more interesting than useful.” [Patient] 18

Patients appreciate when it is acknowledged that their pain experiences are real, even if they are linked to brain function, helping them feel understood. When PNE content is tailored to the individual's experiences, rather than general, patients seem more likely to accept and engage with it. In that respect, the concept of “dangers in me” (DIMs) is considered valuable, as it can help people recognize potential threats that may be exacerbating their pain, even if those threats are not directly related to the pain source. “I have learnt my DIMS and why they matter to my pelvic pain even though they are not related to my pelvis!” [Patient] 21

## 3.4.4. Synthesized finding 4: A group vs an individual setting should be chosen according to patient needs

In synthesized finding 4, it is described that the delivery of PNE through individual or group sessions should be chosen carefully according to the patient's needs. A significant number of patients appreciate PNE content delivered in a group setting, as it provides additional motivation and support through shared experiences. However, some patients struggle to recall specific details from group PNE sessions, with some remembering only vague or minimal aspects of the content. In addition, some patients find group PNE sessions tiring and mentally draining, feeling that the duration (eg, 1.5 hours) is too long, despite finding the session useful. “Together it seems to give another cheer.” [Patient] 14 “It is useful of course, you always remember something, some small things, but 1.5 hours is just too long.” [Patient] 36

## 3.4.5. Synthesized finding 5: A good clinician–patient relationship is crucial for the acceptance of pain neuroscience education

In synthesized finding 5, we reflect on the significance of a good clinician–patient relationship for the use and acceptance of PNE. HCPs are more likely to explain pain mechanisms when they like the patient, with personal rapport influencing their engagement in the conversation. On the other hand, patients appreciate HCPs who actively listen to their narrative, seeking a comprehensive understanding of their experience. When patients feel heard, they feel taken seriously, which allows HCPs to understand their situation better. “I like it when it's a nice person. So, I can take a lot if I have a soft spot for the person in some way. And the next one, pffff.” [General Practitioner] 9 “I was able to tell from my own perspective how something feels, because I felt heard. I felt that I was taken seriously. And when I get that feeling, the other one (healthcare professionals) can get a clearer image of me.” [Patient] 57

## 3.4.6. Synthesized finding 6: Lacking confidence, unclear responsibilities, and a professional culture focused on biomedical aspects of care can keep HCPs from delivering pain neuroscience education

In synthesized finding 6, it is highlighted how HCPs' cognitions, practices, aspects of their professional culture, and a lack of interprofessional collaboration can affect the delivery of PNE. HCPs can be concerned about missing severe somatic conditions, which can make them hesitant to explore specific therapeutic paths. This fear stems from past experiences of not identifying critical issues. Moreover, physiotherapists often find it challenging to explain the bio-psycho-social model of pain to clients. In that respect, physiotherapists believe they lack appropriate tools or scales to assess the psychological aspects of chronic pain, relying mostly on communication with their patients instead. “So, the experience at that time, it shook me that we all just couldn't find it and it were dramatic neurological symptoms and she died, at 62 or something. So that inhibits you a little, to dare to go down that path.” [General Practitioner] 9 “For me, psychological factors can affect the pain overall. As for tools or scales, I honestly have no idea if there is a tool or scale to assess these, except by just communicating with the patient.” [Physiotherapist] 34

There is ambiguity around who should handle pain education's psychological and social aspects. Physiotherapists believe that it is easier to focus on physical treatments, but some believe it is within their scope to address psychological factors affecting pain. ## 4.1. Strengths and limitations

This review demonstrates several methodological strengths that enhance the trustworthiness and relevance of its findings. First, we adhered to the JBI methodology for qualitative meta-aggregation. This ensured a systematic and transparent study appraisal, data extraction, and synthesis. The rigorous assessment of methodological quality using the JBI Critical Appraisal Tool—independently conducted by 2 reviewers—reinforced the credibility of the included studies. Furthermore, an accompanying published protocol contributed to the transparency and accountability of the review process.

Another notable strength is the inclusion of both patient and healthcare provider perspectives. This dual-perspective approach supports a more holistic understanding of how PNE is perceived and applied in clinical contexts. Moreover, the strict inclusion criteria, which focused specifically on educational interventions targeting a reconceptualization of pain from a biopsychosocial perspective, allowed for a high level of specificity in addressing the review questions. Applying the ConQual approach further strengthened the confidence in the synthesized findings by transparently evaluating their credibility and dependability.

Some limitations should be considered when interpreting this review's findings. This review did not include grey literature such as expert commentaries, editorials, or unpublished reports. As a result, potentially rich sources of experiential knowledge and emerging clinical insights may have been overlooked, which could limit the breadth of perspectives captured. The exclusion of specific patient populations, including pediatric, postsurgical, cancer-related, and pregnancy-related chronic pain groups, further narrows the scope of applicability. Consequently, the generalizability of the findings may be limited to the adult population living with musculoskeletal pain in nonpalliative and nonemergency care contexts. Moreover, the transferability of our findings is limited by the fact that the included studies predominantly reflected perspectives of physiotherapists, exercise physiologists, and general practitioners, which may not fully capture the views, implementation contexts, or educational practices of other healthcare professions involved in pain management.

While meta-aggregation offers a structured and policy-relevant synthesis of findings, it does not pursue theory development to the same extent as other approaches, such as meta-ethnography. Thus, this review prioritizes documenting our original interpretations over generating novel theoretical insights. Finally, although studies were drawn from diverse healthcare settings and geographical regions, contextual differences in healthcare delivery, professional roles, and cultural understandings of pain may introduce heterogeneity. When transferring the findings to specific clinical or cultural contexts, these factors should be considered. 45 Thus, this review prioritizes documenting our original interpretations over generating novel theoretical insights. Considering future directions, further work is needed to develop and evaluate PNE approaches tailored to individuals with strongly ingrained biomedical beliefs, as well as to investigate how clinician learning strategies may facilitate shifts toward a biopsychosocial framework. Comparative evaluations of individual vs group-based delivery formats, supported by implementation-focused methodologies, would help to determine how contextual factors influence PNE engagement and feasibility. Moreover, experimental studies are needed to examine the role of the clinician–patient relationship as a mechanistic contributor to PNE delivery. Given that the existing qualitative evidence predominantly reflects the perspectives of physiotherapists, exercise physiologists, and general practitioners, future studies should capture the views of a broader range of healthcare professions to enhance the applicability of findings across clinical settings.

## 5. Conclusion

In this review, the qualitative evidence—rated as low to very low in certainty—provides only tentative insights into factors that may influence the use and acceptance of PNE. Several studies described that communication approaches such as metaphors, repetition, and personally relevant explanations might support understanding, although the consistency and impact of these strategies remain uncertain. Persistent biomedical beliefs were reported as a potential source of skepticism toward PNE, yet the extent to which addressing these beliefs affects engagement is unclear. Preferences regarding delivery format and the perceived quality of the clinician–patient relationship were also noted as possible influences. On the provider-level, including limited confidence, role ambiguity, and adherence to biomedical models, were mentioned as potential barriers to integrating PNE into routine practice. Overall, these themes should be interpreted with caution, and further rigorous research is needed to clarify the mechanisms that influence the implementation of PNE into clinical contexts.
