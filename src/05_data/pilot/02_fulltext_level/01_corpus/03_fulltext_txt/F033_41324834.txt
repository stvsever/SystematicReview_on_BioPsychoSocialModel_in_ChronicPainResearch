TITLE: Expanding the Lens: Recent Developments at the Intersection of Pediatric Chronic Pain and Gender Diversity

ABSTRACT:
This report aims to synthesize the literature at the intersection of pediatric chronic pain and gender diversity, highlighting recent conceptual and empirical developments.
Recent theoretical frameworks expand upon the biopsychosocial model by integrating minority stress, ecological-systems, and intersectional perspectives to better understand the chronic pain experiences of transgender and gender-diverse (TGD) youth. While TGD youth experience disproportionate pain-related risks, influenced by intersecting identity-based stressors, structural inequities, and systemic barriers to affirming care, this group remains underrepresented in pain research and intervention development. To promote equitable care, pain researchers and clinicians must adopt inclusive assessment tools, enhance provider education, implement strengths-based interventions, and advocate for policies that reduce disparities and affirm the identities of TGD youth with pain.
Promoting gender-affirming, trauma-informed, and intersectional approaches may offer a clear path toward more inclusive and equitable pediatric pain management.

## Introduction

Chronic pain, persistent or recurring pain lasting more than three months [ ], is an impactful problem in childhood and adolescence. Prevalence rates reported in epidemiologic reviews vary across studies and by pain presentation. A recent systematic review [ ] reported that chronic pain affects 20.8% of youth, with the highest prevalence for headache and musculoskeletal pain (25.7%). Youth experiencing chronic pain are at increased risk for anxiety, depression, pain interference, sleep disturbance, school and social impairment, functional disability, and reduced quality of life [ – ]. The collective impact of pain and mental health symptoms has a synergistic influence upon youth functioning [ ], with the impact of psychological functioning exceeding that of pain intensity [ ].

The development and persistence of chronic pain in childhood and adolescence is widely understood through a biopsychosocial lens. This model posits that pain is shaped by the interplay of biological processes, psychological functioning, and sociocultural context [ , ]. Consideration of these dimensions is needed to fully understand the pain experience and create a comprehensive, individualized pain management plan. Among these, sociodemographic and social identities (e.g., race/ethnicity, sex, gender identity, sexual orientation) can contribute to variability in pain vulnerability, presentation, assessment, conceptualization, and treatment [ , ].

Despite the multidimensional perspective of the biopsychosocial model, understanding the experiences of transgender and gender-diverse (TGD) youth living with pain remains underdeveloped and underrepresented in pediatric pain literature [ , ]. Of note, TGD youth report chronic pain more often than their cisgender counterparts, with increased stressors believed to magnify the odds of experiencing pain [ ]. More TGD youth have also been presenting for care in tertiary chronic pain clinics in recent years [ ]. However, pain research [ – ] has only just begun to identify the distinctive experiences faced by this group. This review aims to synthesize the current literature at the intersection of pediatric pain and gender diversity, focusing on recent conceptual and empirical developments. We aim to (a) present a conceptual framework that incorporates biopsychosocial, minority stress, ecological-systems, and intersectional models to describe the pain experience in TGD youth, (b) summarize current epidemiology, existing disparities, and evidence for unique pain mechanisms among TGD youth, and (c) offer systemic recommendations for gender-affirming, trauma-informed care going forward. Exploring the experiences of TGD youth with pain and acknowledging the structural forces that shape those experiences can help guide pediatric pain clinicians and researchers toward more equitable and inclusive care.

The 2023 U.S. Youth Risk Behavior Survey [ ] found that 3.3% of high school youth self-identified as transgender and 2.2% identified as [gender]-questioning. A national cohort study [ ] reported that 6.3% of youth (ages 10–17) identified as transgender, gender non-binary, or other diverse gender identity. Other studies report up to 10% of youth identifying as gender-diverse, with non-binary youth typically representing about one-third of the broader TGD population [ , ]. Importantly, prevalence rates for gender diversity also vary by race/ethnicity [ ] and development phase [ ]. This variability underscores the importance of recognizing diversity within TGD youth.

## Evolving Conceptual Frameworks

For TGD youth, the biopsychosocial model alone may be insufficient to capture the complex sociocultural realities that influence the development, persistence, and management of chronic pain. Understanding how and why TGD youth may be at risk for increased pain burden requires a conceptual framework that integrates all aspects of one’s identity, as well as the structural and systems-level factors that affect one’s lived experience. Such factors may not only shape identity development and pain itself, but they may also impact whether TGD youth will access pain treatment at all. Here, we present evolving frameworks that capture the intersecting influences on pain in this population.

## Gender Minority Stress & Resilience Model

The gender minority stress and resilience (GMSR) model [ , ], which draws heavily upon the broader minority stress model [ ], can help to understand the impact of structural and systems-level factors upon TGD youth. This GMSR model explores how chronic exposure to both distal/external (e.g., discrimination, victimization, rejection, gender non-affirmation) and proximal/internal (e.g., anticipated rejection, hiding one’s identity) stressors related to one’s gender identity can impact physical and emotional outcomes [ , ]. While these models are well-established as salient to gender minority populations broadly [ ], limited research has directly applied the model to TGD youth with pain. Further, while the GMSR model provides insight into how minority stress affects individual health outcomes, it does not fully account for the layered contexts that shape those outcomes.

## Ecological Systems Model

Boerner and colleagues [ ] proposed a comprehensive, ecological systems model to better situate TGD youth with chronic pain within their dynamic contexts. Their model was adapted from Bakhshaie and colleagues’ [ ] framework delineating psychosocial predictors of pain and relevant sociocultural determinants in Black individuals. Boerner and colleagues’ [ ] revised model blends socio-ecological theory [ ] with the biopsychosocial model, while incorporating the influences of gender [ , ] and minority stress [ , , ] on pain. As detailed in Fig. , “socio-ecological factors at individual, relational, and societal levels influence the development of pain. These factors influence psychosocial responses to pain, add to cumulative stress, and may diminish coping capacity and perpetuate pain-related disability” [ ]. Conversely, all factors can also promote resilience (e.g., affirming communities, supportive allyship), making the latter a key component of comprehensive pain treatment. In this model, TGD youth are viewed as embedded in interdependent systems that compound or buffer pain experiences. For example, youth with affirming parents may demonstrate better pain coping, even in a hostile school environment. Alternatively, supportive peers may not be enough to mitigate the effects of repeated misgendering in medical settings on willingness to engage in pain treatment. ## Intersectionality

A key strength of an ecological-systems model is its ability to accommodate intersecting identities. Intersectionality recognizes that no single aspect of one’s identity operates in isolation. Using an intersectional lens within this framework offers insight into how such identities individually and collectively shape how pain is expressed, perceived, and treated. This is critical, as many TGD youth navigate several marginalized identities, including those related to race/ethnicity, sexual orientation, socioeconomic status, immigration/refugee status, neurodiversity, and disability status. For instance, a Latine non-binary teen with abdominal pain may be impacted by anti-immigrant bias, racism in healthcare, and language barriers in addition to gender-related distress, while accessing pain care. Similarly, a Black transgender adolescent with pelvic pain may encounter misdiagnosis or dismissal shaped by racial bias, gender nonconformity, and limited clinician training, all of which may influence pain trajectory.

## Underlying Risks & Mechanisms Shaping Pain in TGD Youth

Although TGD youth have gained increased social visibility in recent years [ ], they continue to face a range of intersecting risks that contribute to chronic pain. These include early adversity, psychological distress, physical health disparities, healthcare barriers, and sociopolitical marginalization, each of which may serve as a mechanism for the development and maintenance of chronic pain. Below, we outline these key contributors, highlighting both overlapping vulnerabilities and emerging research that underscores the need for inclusive, affirming, and trauma-informed care.

## Early Adversity and Trauma

TGD youth experience disproportionately high rates of adversity both early in life and as a function of systemic stressors, which have long been associated with poor mental and physical health. Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are well-established predictors of chronic pain [ ], with more than 80% of adolescents with chronic pain report a lifetime history of at least one adverse event [ ]. Notably, ACEs are elevated among TGD youth [ ] and youth exposed to discrimination based on gender identity and/or sexual orientation demonstrate double the risk of chronic pain [ ]. Chronic exposure to minority stress (e.g., rejection, discrimination, invalidation) can directly modulate pain via dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and increased inflammation, while pain-associated internalizing symptoms and social withdrawal can indirectly worsen pain [ , ]. Moreover, TGD high school students report higher rates of violence exposure, unstable housing, school disconnection, and chronic absenteeism due to safety concerns, compared to cisgender peers [ ]. TGD youth are also more likely to live in poverty and experience food insecurity [ ]. Such structural adversity contributes to chronic stress and heightened allostatic load, which in turn dysregulates neuroendocrine, immune, and pain systems [ – ].

## Psychological Distress and Emotional Contributors

Psychological distress is a well-documented mechanism contributing to chronic pain, and TGD youth face particularly elevated risk. They report significantly higher rates of depression, anxiety, insomnia, and suicidal ideation than cisgender and sexual minority peers [ , ]. Non-binary youth face unique psychosocial stressors, including heightened internalizing symptoms and self-harm [ , ], which may further intensify pain vulnerability. These mental health conditions are not only comorbid with chronic pain but also exacerbate pain through cognitive-affective pathways, including catastrophizing, hypervigilance, and reduced coping capacity. Intersectional marginalization compounds this risk, increasing emotional distress and diminishing access to protective resources [ ].

## Physical Health Challenges and Pain Vulnerability

Physical health disparities among TGD youth may be less frequently studied than mental health disparities, but growing evidence reveals clear links to pain-related mechanisms. TGD youth report lower overall health, increased sleep disruption, reduced physical activity, and significantly poorer health-related quality of life [ , ] compared to cisgender youth. These disruptions to physical activity and restorative sleep can lead to deconditioning and increased pain. Co-occurring cognitive, physical, or psychiatric disabilities can further intensify these disparities [ ].

Some pain experiences may arise directly from efforts to affirm one’s gender. Chest binding practices, for instance, are commonly associated with increased back, chest, and shoulder pain [ , ]. Emerging research also suggests complex associations between gender-affirming hormone therapies and pain, given the underlying shifts in hormonal regulation of pain processing. Testosterone may reduce pre-existing pain in transmasculine individuals, while estrogen or antiandrogen may lead to new-onset pain in transfeminine individuals [ , ], although causal mechanisms remain unclear. In their qualitative study with TGD youth, Boerner and colleagues [ ] identified a theme describing tension between affirming one’s gender and managing their pain, pertaining to binding, hormones, and gender-affirming surgery. Some participants framed such pain as meaningful or empowering; others described distress from having to choose between competing pain sources.

## Barriers To Affirming and Inclusive Health Care

TGD youth face significant barriers to accessing competent, affirming health care. Experiences of misgendering, discrimination, and non-affirmation in healthcare settings can elevate distress, foster distrust, and discourage help-seeking, all barriers that can disrupt adequate assessment and treatment [ , , ]. These barriers are particularly consequential for youth with chronic pain, as delays in care are associated with increased pain chronicity and continued disability [ ].

Structural challenges, such as lack of provider training, cisnormative assessment tools, restrictions on insurance coverage, and reporting obligations, further limit access to supportive health care [ , ]. Providers may lack training in the needs of TGD patients. Even providers who express affirming attitudes often report limited confidence in their knowledge and preparedness to support TGD patients [ ]. This increases the likelihood of negative interactions and inadequate care. Many TGD youth report having to educate their providers about their gender identity and medical needs, a burden that also can hinder engagement in care [ ]. Further, healthcare systems often rely on outdated and cisnormative tools (e.g., binary body maps, items that conflate sex and gender, forced choice response options, items collapsing diverse identities into an “Other” category). These tools not only impede accurate pain assessment but can compound stigma and invisibility already experienced by many TGD youth in medical settings [ , ]. Best-practice guidelines [ ] have been developed for inclusive, affirming assessment of gender identity, along with sexual orientation; pain scientists and providers are called upon to integrate these approaches [ , ].

Some TGD youth choose not to disclose their gender identity in clinical settings, due to a belief that gender is irrelevant to their pain care, internalized stigma, or lack of opportunity to share [ ]. TGD youth may also fear the consequences of disclosing their gender identity when seeking care, including the potential for disbelief, rejection, discrimination, or undesired disclosure to others [ ]. However, excluding consideration of gender identity from assessment and treatment undermines holistic care. In particular, TGD youth with pain often find themselves fighting to legitimize both their pain and their gender and feeling forced to navigate double the stigma in systems not designed to recognize or fully support either identity [ ]. Invalidation of either identity can heighten distress, interfere with adherence, and amplify pain [ , ].

## Sociopolitical Climate

Individual risk factors and systemic barriers do not exist in a vacuum. They are shaped and often intensified by the broader sociopolitical climate, which has become increasingly hostile to TGD youth in recent years (especially in the U.S.). The U.S. has witnessed a notable escalation in legislation being proposed, passed, and enacted that impacts the rights of TGD youth and LGBTQ + youth broadly (Redfield et al., 2024). Recent executive orders have further restricted access to gender-affirming medical care (e.g., puberty blockers, hormones, surgical interventions), limited the use of chosen names and pronouns in medical settings, and sanctioned provider refusal based on personal beliefs [ ]. The vast majority of TGD youth in the U.S. live in states that have passed or proposed broader laws banning access participation in sports, use of bathrooms and sex-separated facilities, and pronoun use, as well as the forced outing of gender identity (Redfield et al., 2024).

Such policies significantly increase distress for TGD youth and allied providers. The overarching climate contributes to “ambient threat,” a chronic backdrop of fear and hypervigilance among marginalized youth [ ]. For youth with pain, this can elicit the physiological changes to the nervous system that trigger and exacerbate pain. Further, such political barriers represent not only legal obstacles; they are powerful signals of societal invalidation. This can inadvertently reinforce distrust in healthcare systems, which for many TGD youth with pain, are already difficult to navigate. Such policies also have the potential to erode school connectedness and increase social isolation, which correlates with greater pain intensity and disability [ ].

## Emerging Evidence at the Intersection of Pain & Gender Identity

Until recently, understanding the chronic pain experience of TGD youth was limited to case reports, chart reviews, and descriptive case-series analyses, with mixed results. More recently, a cross-sectional study of youth with chronic pain [ ] found no significant differences in functional disability, symptom severity, quality of life, or pain-related attitudes and beliefs between gender-diverse youth and matched cisgender youth. However, the relationship between pain intensity and quality of life was stronger for gender-diverse youth. Conversely, a retrospective chart review of 140 youth with pain [ ] found that cisgender youth reported worse pain intensity, while TGD patients reported lower quality of life and more internalizing symptoms than their counterparts. The relationships between pain intensity and pain outcomes (e.g., disability, quality of life, catastrophizing) did not differ between groups. Similarly, a recent case series analysis [ ] described significantly higher distress and impairment (e.g., depressive symptoms, functional disability, pain-related fear) in TGD youth (vs. matched cisgender youth) with pain. Given the small samples and inconsistencies in findings, larger trials using mixed methodology are needed to contextualize these observations.

Several additional studies have emerged among TGD youth engaging in intensive interdisciplinary pain treatment (IIPT) programs for functional restoration. For example, Mulvihill and colleagues [ ] reported that the percentage of adolescents entering IIPT identifying as gender-diverse was higher than the prevalence of gender-diverse youth in the general population. In this study, non-binary youth reported the lowest pain acceptance, highest pain interference, and highest levels of anxiety, while transgender youth reported the highest pain acceptance [ ]. Similarly, in a chart review of nearly 500 youth admitted to IIPT [ ], more than 6% identified as TGD and reported higher emotional distress and functional impairment at baseline (vs. matched cisgender peers). Further, TGD youth reported significant improvements at discharge (comparable to cisgender peers), but they were significantly less likely to complete the program. Understanding the experiences of TGD youth in IIPT is one important aspect of exploring the intersection of gender diversity and pain. However, IIPT is a high level of tertiary pain care; such studies reflect the experiences of those referred to and resourced to attend such programs, reflecting a specific subset of TGD youth with pain.

Recently Boerner and colleagues (2025) qualitatively explored the lived experiences of TGD youth with pain and noted that the impact of intersecting identities was undeniable. Of note, many interviewees indicated that holding a neurodiverse identity was a key component of their pain experience, consistent with the heightened co-occurrence rate of Autism Spectrum Disorder [ ] and Attentive Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder [ ] among TGD youth. Regardless of formal diagnosis, TGD youth also endorse higher levels of autistic traits, systemizing, and sensory sensitivity than cisgender peers [ ]. However, neurodiverse youth are not well-represented in chronic pain literature and studies examining the relationships between neurodiversity and pain are heterogeneous and generally low quality [ ].

## Recommendations for Equity and Inclusivity in Pediatric Pain

The growing but limited body of research underscores the need for inclusive, culturally to chronic pain care for TGD youth, many of whom have complex, intersecting identities. Both pain researchers and clinicians must adopt and advocate for equitable, identity-affirming approaches, grounded in the frameworks and findings above. ## Adopting Inclusive & Intersectional Assessment Practices

Clinicians, researchers, and advocates for systemic change must embrace the experiences of all youth living with pain across the spectrum of gender identities, in concert with their intersecting social positions. Recommendations to facilitate inclusive assessment emphasize the importance of using developmentally appropriate, identity-affirming approaches when collecting data on gender identity in pediatric pain research and clinical care. Key themes include avoiding binary or “othering” categorizations, allowing for fluidity and self-identification, and updating tools and language to reflect best practices and the diversity of experiences. These strategies not only provide a more affirming experience for patients and study participants, but they also may offer researchers and clinicians a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of the pain experience.

## Designing Inclusive & Intersectional Research

To address their historical exclusion, research on TGD youth and chronic pain should prioritize methods that center and amplify their voices [ ]. Key themes include employing qualitative and participatory methods, grounding studies in intersectional frameworks, and intentionally including underrepresented populations to capture the full complexity of pain experiences. By prioritizing patient safety, being thoughtful in research design, and incorporating lived experience perspectives, researchers can foster safe spaces for engagement in health care and related research endeavors.

## Enhancing Provider Knowledge & Clinical Experience

Increasing the knowledge base and clinical experience of pediatric providers in providing culturally responsive pain care is key to reducing disparities for TGD youth. Clinicians working with youth in pain (across all relevant disciplines) require dedicated training and concrete tools to offer inclusive, gender affirming care. Central themes include integrating gender-affirming content into medical and professional curricula, promoting ongoing education on intersectionality and trauma-informed care, and equipping providers to support youth and their families. Offering continued opportunities for professional development allows providers to evolve their practice with an evolving world.

## Affirming Gender Identity

Despite the health disparities experienced by TGD youth, with social support, gender affirmation, and appropriate health care, TGD youth can thrive. Gender affirmation refers to the process of reflection, acceptance, and/or intervention to better align one’s gender expression with their gender identity [ ]. This process occurs intra-personally (within oneself) and interpersonally (in relation to others). Recent research suggests TGD youth who are affirmed in their gender identity socially (Olson, 2016) and medically [ ] demonstrate significant improvement in health outcomes. Given the relationship between psychological distress and chronic pain, pain in TGD youth may improve when their identity is affirmed socially and/or medically. In the health care setting, providers can demonstrate their commitment to gender-affirmation by respecting chosen names and pronouns, ensuring inclusive language and diverse representation on materials, and fostering a welcoming atmosphere through visual cues and gender-neutral facilities.

## Trauma-informed Clinical Approaches

Most interventions for pediatric chronic pain were not developed with TGD youth in mind. However, care that acknowledges the impact of adversity may have the potential to ameliorate risk for poor health outcomes and reduce barriers to care in TGD youth. Trauma-informed care (TIC) is defined as care in which all parties involved assess, recognize, and respond to the effects of traumatic stress on youth, caregivers, and health care providers [ ]. TIC offers a unifying approach that emphasizes emotional safety, empowerment, choice, and cultural humility [ , ], an ideal fit for TGD youth. In the context of pain, trauma-informed approaches recognize the impact of past medical or identity-centered trauma on current pain symptoms, while simultaneously creating clinical environments where TGD youth with pain feel seen, respected, and affirmed [ ]. Given that TGD youth may experience adversity and trauma at multiple levels of their environment, TIC is critical for effective pain management.

## Strengths-based Interventions

Although acknowledging adversity faced by TGD youth with pain remains essential, an exclusively deficit-based perspective may overlook strengths and capacity for resilience [ ]. Resilience is described as the ability to survive and thrive in the face of adversity. For TGD youth, resilience can be influenced by belonging to a community, having supportive allyship, and experiencing gender euphoria. These can protect against many negative health outcomes that result from minority stressors [ ]. Pain researchers and clinicians can help TGD youth to identify and cultivate sources of resilience. For instance, gender euphoria, the joy and contentment experienced when one’s gender expression aligns with their internal sense of gender, is recognized as a powerful tool in promoting well-being. Pain providers can incorporate identity-affirming practices that elicit gender euphoria (e.g., pronoun respect, social affirmation, connecting youth to affirming peer communities) to serve as a buffer against distress and pain amplification in TGD youth. These interventions, though small, can offer psychological empowerment and self-regulation, which in turn can mitigate pain. Larger-scale interventions promoting resilience are also needed. For instance, researchers developed an eight-week, peer-support, virtual wellness intervention for TGD adults, Trans-Wellness , , Trans-Brillance , designed to enhance resilience, capacity, and emotional well-being through community [ , designed to enhance resilience, capacity, and emotional well-being through community [ ]. Creating a similar, developmentally appropriate group for TGD youth with pain would be invaluable. As Goffnett & Paceley [ ] noted, TGD youth often encourage peers to persevere, feel proud of their identities, and support their wellbeing through connections to others.

## Systems-level Advocacy & Policy Change

A growing body of literature emphasizes the importance of fostering contextual resilience in addition to individual resilience in TGD youth living with pain. This requires that researchers and clinicians work as advocates for change, challenging and dismantling oppressive systems. Chronic pain is a pressing public health concern, and recent legislation targeting TGD youth poses direct threats to their pain trajectories and overall well-being. Within an ecological framework, such policies at the systemic level compound individual, relational, and societal barriers to effective, inclusive pain management. Therefore, systems-level advocacy, collaboration, and investment are needed to effect change toward affirming, equity-driven care for all TGD youth with chronic pain. Ideally, this would include promoting protective policies, eliminating barriers to gender-affirming health services, disseminating best practice guidelines, and funding research to address disparities in access and outcomes for TGD youth with pain.

## Conclusion

TGD youth face unique and compounding vulnerabilities that heighten their risk for chronic pain and adverse outcomes. Integrating biopsychosocial, minority stress, and ecological-systems frameworks reveals how structural inequities interact with interpersonal and intrapersonal pain mechanisms, contributing to disproportionate health burdens. Still, TGD youth remain underrepresented in pediatric pain literature, leaving critical gaps in understanding, assessment, and treatment. Still, the field is evolving; emerging research is beginning to center TGD voices, highlight risk and resilience, and propose models that reflect the complexity of identity, adversity, and care access. By embracing intersectional frameworks, delivering gender-affirming, trauma-informed care, and advocating for systemic change, clinicians and researchers can help ensure pain management is not only comprehensive but inclusive and just. With greater visibility, targeted research, and policy reform, meaningful progress for TGD youth with pain is possible.
