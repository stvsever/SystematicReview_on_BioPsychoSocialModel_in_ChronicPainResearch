TITLE: Existential therapy for treating chronic pain: A scoping review

ABSTRACT:
Integrating an existential lens into chronic pain treatment enables a holistic and biopsychosocial approach to treating chronic pain. This scoping review aimed to identify the characteristics, themes, and results of existential therapy for treating chronic pain. We searched seven databases for empirical papers that described an existentially based intervention for a chronic pain sample. Conducted in May 2023, the search terms were related to chronic pain and truncated versions of existential and humanistic therapies. Six articles met the criteria and were included. Quantitative and qualitative studies were included, ranging from randomized controlled trials, non‐randomized experiments, a case report, and a case study. Extracted data included study characteristics and common intervention themes (e.g., meaningfulness, self‐identity, etc.) and outcome themes (e.g., pain‐related, life satisfaction, etc.). All studies reported a reduction in pain symptoms or severity. Physical functioning was generally not influenced by existential therapy; however, it did improve with or in combination with cognitive‐behavioral therapy (CBT). Meaningfulness was a common theme in all the interventions, and most articles reported increased meaningfulness in the outcomes. Spirituality seems to moderate the effectiveness of existential therapy. This offers an overview of the current state of knowledge of academically labeled existential therapies for chronic pain treatment. This further provides suggestions for advancing the study of this topic by highlighting both commonalities and inconsistencies in intervention findings.

## INTRODUCTION

Recently, increased recognition concerning the impact of chronic pain on the existential domains of life has surfaced. Dezutter et al.’s ( ) research has shown that while everyone may experience existential concerns, these concerns tend to expand with the experience of chronic illness or pain. New questions may surface concerning meaningfulness, death anxiety, self‐identity, and loss of freedom (Coren, ; Hajibabaei et al., ; McCabe et al., ). Thus, existentially informed approaches to treating chronic conditions may be useful for addressing existential concerns that would otherwise go unacknowledged, which may include addressing concerns of meaningfulness, death anxiety, acceptance of loss, and impact of pain (Böhmer et al., ; Coren, ; Gebler & Maercker, ; Hajibabaei et al., ; Iglesias, ; McCabe et al., ) While a considerable body of research exists on existential and spiritual factors among end‐of‐life care patients, minimal research has explored these factors in relation to pain medicine (Lovell et al., ). The purpose of this paper is to characterize research efforts on existential approaches to treating people with chronic pain. This review enables the description of existential therapeutic intervention prevalence, identifies commonly employed methods, and highlights common themes within and between the existing approaches.

## UNDERSTANDING EXISTENTIALISM IN RELATION TO EXISTING PAIN THERAPIES

Existentialism is a philosophical movement that originated in continental Europe and centers around the individual human's experience (Iglesias, ). An existentialist perspective recognizes individuals as architects of their own lives with the capacity and freedom to form their destiny (Iglesias, ), essentially working to reallocate power to the individual (De Siqueira, ).

## Existentialism and pain

In a study conducted by Anderson et al. ( ), eight physicians who treat chronic pain were interviewed. The researchers found that most of these physicians found it challenging to address their patients' existential needs, reporting that these issues felt too personal to directly address, that their medical training did not equip them to provide existential care, that it was outside of their roles, or that they did not have adequate time to address these concerns. These are gaps within the medical system that can be complemented with a structured existential therapeutic framework designed to address existential issues.

While traditional approaches to treating pain tend to rely on a bio‐medical care approach, which views the body as distinct and unrelated components, there has been a more recent shift toward the biopsychosocial approach, viewing health as an interaction between body, mind, and environment (Anderson et al., ; Lima et al., ). In line with this biopsychosocial approach is person‐centered treatment, a type of therapy operating on the humanistic belief that the client inherently has the capacity and drive for growth and self‐actualization (Yao & Kabir, ). Since existentialism's conceptualization as a philosophy, it has been adopted as a person‐centered therapeutic perspective within humanistic psychotherapies (Iglesias, ). Existential therapy for chronic pain is based on an accommodative coping approach, which focuses on one's flexibility in adapting personal aims and attitudes to current circumstances (Brandstädter & Gerolf, ; Gebler & Maercker, ). For example, Gebler and Maercker ( ) demonstrate that one main purpose of chronic pain existential therapy is the acceptance of pain as well as the acceptance of other physical symptoms, unwanted emotional experiences, or incriminating thoughts. This is fueled by the existential perspective of recognizing the importance of facing and accepting the reality of suffering.

Chronic pain can have a substantial impact on identity and self‐perception (Reed et al., ; Smith & Osborne, ). For example, individuals with chronic pain must often reframe how they relate to their body and their pain, which may include making adjustments to how they incorporate the concept of their body into their sense of identity, how they rely on and are aware of their body, and how they understand its pain (Afrell et al., ). The process of being forced to make these mental adjustments in response to chronic pain can result in existential challenges in finding a new way to relate to oneself (Andersen et al., ). Significant correlations have established relationships between death anxiety, pain acceptance, and pain‐related disability (Reed et al., ). Self‐concept clarity has also been found to be correlated with pain disability and severity (Reed et al., ). These relationships emphasize both the susceptibility of populations with chronic pain in facing existential concerns such as death anxiety and self‐identity, as well as highlight the potential value in addressing these concerns.

Another requisite for holistic health includes the search for existential meaning in life, which may in turn foster a sense of autonomy and responsibility in the individual (De Siqueira, ). Meaning in life, specifically one's ability to make sense of their lives, is significantly associated with chronic pain outcomes (Boring et al., ). Research shows that the belief that one is living a meaningful life is associated with less pain interference (Reed et al., ). Pain can often alter or impact one's sense of meaning in life, triggering existential frustrations and anxieties (Lovell et al., ). In a study assessing existential needs among patients living with chronic kidney disease, patients reported needing support in finding hope, finding a present meaning in life, dying and death, and finding peace of mind (Davison & Jhangri, ). Further, those living with persistent pain or cancer tend to report low levels of meaning and high levels of depression (Lovell et al., ). Maintaining existential well‐being through exploring and re‐affirming the meaning of one's life is an important process for those living with pain symptoms (De Siqueira, ).

## Existentialism and cognitive‐behavioral therapy (CBT)

Different from the accommodative therapeutic approach of existential therapy, CBT is an assimilative problem‐oriented approach that is typically used to recognize and change distressing and false thoughts (Institute for Quality and Efficiency in Healthcare, ). CBT has been effectively applied as a treatment for varying psychosocial disorders, notably depression and anxiety, as well as for treating symptoms associated with chronic pain (Stewart et al., ). For use with chronic pain patients, CBT largely targets pain reduction strategies and has been proven effective in reducing pain outcomes (Yong et al., ). Like existential therapy, CBT may also be useful in addressing certain symptoms associated with chronic pain by helping patients navigate common concerns of anxiety surrounding death, freedom, and meaning (Heidenreich et al., ). CBT is a well‐established therapy for the treatment of chronic pain (Edhe et al., ; Hylands‐White et al., ). However, it has also been argued that using a CBT approach for the treatment of chronic pain symptoms may be inadequate in helping patients address certain existential issues, like the meaning of life and death (Heidenreich et al., ). Further, chronic pain treatment effect sizes tend to be small to moderate and therefore have room for improvement, and research suggests that CBT is not equally effective among all populations (Boderick et al., ). Consequently, CBT methods have expanded through new variations, such as third‐wave accommodative approaches like acceptance and commitment therapies, mindfulness‐based cognitive therapy (Day, ), and hypnotic‐based cognitive therapy (Jensen et al., ). In an effort to further explore alternate methods to holistically address patient needs, researchers such as Gebler and Maercker ( ) recommend a combined approach of CBT and existential therapy. However, due to the differences in theoretical underpinnings of existential and CBT approaches, there has historically been minimal overlap between the two (Heidenreich et al., ). Nevertheless, an increase in existential concerns being treated with CBT has led to integrative training, and Heidenreich et al. ( ) argue for the large potential that exists at the crossroads of existential psychotherapy and CBT. Gebler and Maercker ( ) spearheaded this approach in a randomized control trial in which they combined CBT and existential therapeutic methods for chronic pain participants and found that the integration of existential methods was related to lower levels of pain‐related disability. Through combining the approaches, further exploration of common questions surrounding death, freedom, and meaning may be accomplished while simultaneously providing tools to better manage anxiety and depression (Heidenreich et al., ).

## RATIONALE FOR CURRENT RESEARCH

Understanding certain existential concerns that may be triggered by chronic pain is most adequately supported through a biopsychosocial approach to care, which can incorporate existential well‐being (De Siqueira, ). Existential needs are often inadequately supported in those living with chronic pain, and there remains room for improvement in what is currently known about how to address these needs (Grassi et al., ). To the best of our knowledge, there are no scoping reviews assessing the current state of knowledge of treatments for chronic pain that rely on existential principles. As such, the present study asks, what characteristics and outcomes are associated with using existential approaches to treating populations with chronic pain?

## METHODS

This scoping review was conducted following the JBI methodology for scoping reviews (Peters et al., ). Scoping reviews address an exploratory research question by systematically searching, selecting, and synthesizing a wide range of literature to determine the breadth of evidence on a particular topic (Peters et al., ). They are a type of knowledge synthesis that scopes or maps a body of literature with relevance to time, location, source, method, and origin (Levac et al., ).

## Search Strategy

For the search strategy, search terms were used that relate to chronic pain (e.g., chronic pain, continuous pain, persistent pain, long‐term pain) and truncated versions of existential and humanistic therapies (e.g., existential*, humanist*) to identify all relevant studies that explore existential therapies for chronic pain. In May 2023, seven databases were searched: PsycINFO (1806–2023), Web of Science (1900–2023), CINAHL (−2023), Philosopher's Index (1940–2023), PhilPapers (−2023), Medline (Ovid; 1946–2022), and PubMed (−2023). The result of the search is reported in a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) flow diagram (Moher et al., ; Tricco et al., ; see Figure ).

## Eligibility Criteria

In the title and abstract stage, articles were included if studies were conducted on populations with chronic pain through an existential lens. In the full‐text screening stage, inclusion criteria became limited to peer‐reviewed articles that had clearly defined existential interventions and had clear documentation on participants' pain levels or experiences of life pre‐ and post‐intervention. ## Study Selection

Results from the literature search were imported using Covidence, a reference management software (Veritas Health Innovation, ). Duplicate studies were removed, and two reviewers independently screened the records by title and abstract, then by full text. Conflicts in the title and abstract stage were resolved by a third independent reviewer, while disagreements in the full‐text stage were resolved by consensus between the two original reviewers.

## Data Extraction

Pairs of individual reviewers analyzed the included studies and extracted the data using a data extraction tool within the Covidence software. The data extraction included author(s), year of publication, country, participant population demographics, aim of study, study design, intervention details, and outcomes of the study. Disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through consensus. All data were later imported into an Excel sheet for further analysis.

Data extracted from the included articles are presented in a tabular form. Table reports key findings relevant to the review question. Data were synthesized based on interventions and outcomes, which were then classified into themes using content analysis. Through this process, researchers reflexively examined and interpreted article content to identify patterns, themes, and concepts, aiming to categorically group them in a manner that could provide a snapshot of the details provided in the articles about interventions and participant outcomes. A narrative summary accompanies the tabulated data to describe how the results relate to the research question.

## Study Selection

A database search of the existing literature resulted in the review of 727 studies. After 315 duplicates were removed, 412 articles were screened, of which 341 were deemed irrelevant. This resulted in 71 full‐text studies to screen, of which 65 were excluded (see Figure ). In the end, six articles were left to extract. ## Year of Publication

All articles were published within the past 19 years, with a slight increase over time in the frequency of publications using existential therapies for chronic pain.

## Country

The studies in this review were published from a variety of locations, the most common places being Germany (2/6, 33%) and the United States (2/6, 33%). There were also publications from Australia (1/6, 17%) and Iran (1/6, 17%).

## Study Design and Methods

A variety of study designs were included, with the most common being randomized controlled trial (2/6, 33%) and non‐randomized experimental study (2/6, 33%). The other two studies focused on a smaller population size, one being a case series (1/6, 17%) and the other being a case report (1/6, 17%). Most of the studies were quantitative in data collection and analysis, using questionnaires (4/6, 67%), while the two case studies were qualitative, reviewing therapy sessions (2/6, 33%).

## Measurements

There were several measurements used to record pain data, including self‐report pain intensity measures (3/6, 50%), self‐report pain interference (1/6, 17%), Pain Self‐Efficacy Questionnaire (1/6, 17%), Pain Catastrophizing Scale (1/6, 17%), Modified Roland & Morris Disability Questionnaire (1/6, 17%), Chronic Pain Acceptance Questionnaire (1/6, 17%), and Pain Disability Index (1/6, 17%).

Other measures were used to record information concerning quality of life and life satisfaction, such as the Multiple Sclerosis Quality of Life Questionnaire (1/6, 17%), Meaning in Life Questionnaire (1/6, 17%), Satisfaction with Life Scale (2/6, 33%), Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy – Spiritual Well‐Being Extended (1/6, 17%), and The Sources of Meaning and Meaning in Life Questionnaire (1/6, 17%). Finally, two of the studies assessed participants' mental health with the Depression Anxiety Stress Scale (1/6, 17%) and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (1/6, 17%).

## Age group

Populations within these six studies covered a wide range of age groups. Several studies included participants with mean ages between 50 and 69 years (3/6, 50%). One study included participants only described as being 18 years of age or older (1/6, 18%). The case series included in this scoping review reported that of their three participants, two were older adults and one was a young adult (1/6. 18%), and the case study included in this review reported that the participant was a late adolescent (1/6, 18%).

## Gender

All four of the case study subjects from the two case studies were men (2/6, 33%), whereas Hajibabaei et al. ( ) had 100% women participants in all three groups (1/6, 15%). The other three studies included both men and women (3/6, 50%). McCabe et al. ( ) had 75% women participants, Böhmer et al. ( ) had 62% and 67% women in the experimental and control groups, respectively, and Gebler and Maercker ( ) had 58% and 59% women in the experimental and control groups, respectively.

## Pain Diagnosis/Duration

All participants suffered from chronic pain. The conditions present amongst participants included terminal cancer (1/6, 17%), multiple sclerosis (1/6, 17%), and living with chronic non‐cancer pain (2/6, 33%). Some participants' pain diagnoses were not shared; however, their pain durations were described. The remaining two studies indicated that their participants had been living with chronic pain for about ten years (2/6, 33%).

## Focus of Interventions

Each study included in this scoping review aimed to focus on or improve the participants' sense of meaningfulness (6/6, 100%). Other common themes in the interventions included working on self‐identity and a sense of awareness (3/3, 50%) or addressing and coping with death anxiety and existential isolation (2/6, 33%). Less common intervention themes were freedom of religion and moral values (1/6, 17%), the impact of pain (1/6, 17%), and acceptance of loss and suffering (1/6, 17%).

## Outcomes

All studies reported improvements in pain symptoms (3/6, 50%), pain intensity (3/6, 50%), or self‐perceived disability (2/6, 33%). One study reported improvements in physical functioning (1/6, 17%). Pain‐related factors were also impacted, such as increases in pain acceptance (1/6, 17%) and decreases in pain catastrophizing (1/6, 17%). Increases in meaning in life scores were reported by half of the studies (3/6, 50%). One study did not find any significant changes in meaningfulness; however, it did find that there was a significant decrease in crisis of meaning (1/6, 17%). Life satisfaction levels were found to increase in one study (1/6, 17%), and quality of life was found to improve in another study (1/6, 17%). Significant changes in levels of anxiety, depression, and affective distress were reported by half of the studies (3/6, 50%). Other observed changes include increases in cognitive functioning (1/6, 17%), and increases in levels of self‐efficacy (2/6, 33%). Another common theme was that the participant's levels of spirituality acted as a factor that influenced patient outcomes (2/6, 33%).

## DISCUSSION

This scoping review provides an overview of research published over the previous 19 years on existential therapeutic interventions for populations with chronic pain. Results from this scoping review suggest that there are beneficial effects of incorporating an existential component to chronic pain therapy, which is supported by existing articles that argue for the implementation of existentially focused treatments by explaining their theoretical bases and underpinnings (Grassi et al., ; Lima et al., ). While existential benefits have been recognized within palliative care populations with some improvements documented in existential well‐being, spiritual well‐being, and existential distress (Terao & Satoh, ), the current study focuses more specifically on any relevant outcomes of populations with chronic pain, including existential outcomes and pain‐related outcomes.

All studies reported a reduction of pain symptoms, severity, or disability (Böhmer et al., ; Coren, ; Gebler & Maercker, ; Hajibabaei et al., ; Iglesias, ; McCabe et al., ). These findings suggest that addressing existential needs can provide physical alleviation of pain symptoms, which aligns with the theory suggesting existential intervention may reduce pain by decreasing pain sensitivity (McCabe et al., ) or with the theory that existential treatment works to reduce the negative evaluation of painful experiences (Gebler & Maercker, ).

Interestingly, the intervention that produced quantitative improvements in physical functioning (McCabe et al., ) was a combined existential, spiritual, and cognitive‐behavioral treatment method. When compared to Hajibabaei et al.’s ( ) study, the existential‐spiritual psychotherapy group did not report any physical functioning improvements, but the cognitive‐behavioral group did. While these findings indicate that CBT may be more effective in helping participants' physical functioning levels, they also inform us that these benefits can still be reaped when existential and spiritual perspectives are integrated into a cognitive‐behavioral framework. This is further supported by the qualitative findings of the Coren ( ) study, which described its intervention as a process of integrating existential and psychodynamic methods with CBT.

Meaningfulness or crisis of meaning was observed to improve in most studies (Böhmer et al., ; Coren, ; Hajibabaei et al., ; Iglesias, ), but not all (Gebler & Maercker, ; McCabe et al., ). This is interesting considering that each of the studies' interventions had a meaning‐based component; however, it should be noted that there was a small improvement observed in meaningfulness in the McCabe et al. ( ) study that was not deemed to be statistically significant, and meaningfulness was not measured as an outcome variable in Gebler and Maercker's ( ) study. Addressing meaningfulness concerns is a key aspect of existential therapy (Heidenreich et al., ), and it does not appear to be a common treatment concern among other therapeutic approaches for treating chronic pain. While our findings indicate that using an existential therapeutic approach is beneficial in improving a sense of meaningfulness, it may simply be more effective than other treatment methods due to its willingness and ability to address this existential concern.

When assessing the effectiveness of existential therapeutic interventions, spirituality appears to be a moderator influencing the participants' outcomes (Gebler & Maercker, ; McCabe et al., ). Spirituality was also identified as an intervention theme in Hajibabaei et al.’s ( ) study in which the intervention was created in part in accordance with the Holy Quran. Participants who self‐rated as more spiritual were found to have benefited from the longest, most sustained improvements from the existential interventions, while those who were non‐spiritual and underwent cognitive‐behavioral interventions saw no significant improvements (Gebler & Maercker, ). These findings fall in line with the literature, as measures of spirituality are associated with pain and physical functioning among chronic pain patients (Ferreira‐Valente et al., ), and spiritual development is known to be an adaptive coping strategy in cancer patients (Scrignaro et al., ). It is proposed that, because spirituality and the meaning of life are both nonphysical, transcendental values, spiritual individuals may react differently to existential therapy, finding more value in gaining a deeper and more spiritual understanding of their pain (Gebler & Maercker, ). Knowing that spirituality acts as an influencing factor, self‐related spirituality has the potential to act as a prognostic tool for determining which treatment interventions to employ and how likely they are to reduce patients' physical pain and pain symptoms (McCabe et al., ).

## Limitations and Directions for Future Research

The current research aims to identify existing chronic pain therapies that are explicitly identified as being ‘existential’ in nature. Using the search terms “existential*” and “humanist*” synthesized research that is academically being labeled as such within the literature; however, due to the multifaceted and nuanced definitions of existentialism, it is possible that there are other studies that include themes and philosophies that align with existential therapy that are not explicitly labeled as such. Broadly, interventions of a similar nature may exist with varying terms such as spiritual, client‐centered, patient‐centered, human‐centered, or phenomenological approaches. More specifically, there may also be certain components within therapies that focus on existential concerns. Therefore, future studies may be interested in exploring all types of humanistic therapeutic interventions for coping with chronic pain, and may benefit from including existentially related search terms, such as death, freedom, isolation, meaningfulness, autonomy, and identity, for example.

The exclusion of studies conducted in languages other than English acts as a potential limitation to our scoping review. Due to this limitation, it is likely not all articles of interest were captured. Translating the studies from other languages at the time this scoping review was conducted was not a viable option as it went beyond the scope of the abilities of the reviewers. Therefore, a comprehensive review of published articles in languages other than English is warranted.

This study focuses on populations with chronic pain, excluding chronic disease and chronic illness populations. Future studies should look to expand the search to incorporate wider populations among the chronic pain community.

Finally, our study highlighted the area of existential therapy as under‐researched, producing only six academic, peer‐reviewed articles detailing explicitly existential interventions and data on their efficacy. Within these studies, the majority of the research was conducted with older populations, leaving a gap in the literature concerning younger and middle‐aged populations. Future researchers should aim to implement existential therapeutic methods for younger populations.

## CONCLUSIONS

Interventions beyond CBT are likely needed to address the varied needs of populations with chronic pain. The current study demonstrates the value in addressing existential concerns amongst populations with chronic pain, highlighting the potential for both existential and physical benefits. While only indicated by some of the six included studies, there are provocative and generative findings that indicate that existential therapeutic interventions appear most effective in treating a range of chronic pain symptoms when they are combined with a cognitive‐behavioral therapeutic approach. This justifies the need for further research and clinical trials that combine these therapeutic approaches. If research continues to demonstrate increased benefits for populations with chronic pain when existential therapies and cognitive‐behavioral therapies are combined, future chronic pain interventions may be able to treat even broader patient needs, helping clinicians to treat patients in a more holistic sense. More research in this area is needed, and it is hoped that existential therapies for populations with chronic pain are further developed and evaluated to understand what interventions work and for whom they work. Existential interventions, for example, appear to be more substantial and long‐lasting among spiritual populations. It is hoped that this scoping review offers an overview of existing research and suggestions for progressing the field so that chronic pain interventions can best holistically address the needs of populations with chronic pain.
